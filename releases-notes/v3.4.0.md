# v3.4.0 — 2026-09-07

One epic, six stories, and one question: *what do I cook this week?* Fuelr
could already hold a recipe, plan a day and build a shopping list. What it
could not do was help you decide — and deciding is the part that takes the
Sunday evening.

So the planner now fills itself in a direction you name, lets you argue with
what it proposes, hands you the shopping list at the end, and — for the weeks
you would rather cook once — chooses dishes that share their work and reads
them back to you as one afternoon.

Almost none of it costs anything, and that is a design decision rather than a
happy accident.

## A recipe knows what it is

Two closed lists were added to a recipe: a **cuisine** — Italian, Thai,
Lebanese, twelve in all, at most one per recipe — and the tags it already had.

Closed on purpose. "Fill my week with Italian food" has to be computable, and
it is not if the value is whatever somebody typed: a recipe tagged *soupe* is a
recipe no filter will ever find. Seasons already worked this way, and a cuisine
is the same shape of fact.

The library filters on it, and — this is the point — so does everything below.
An intention is a tag and a cuisine is a column, so "the recipes I already
wrote that match this" is a database query rather than a guess.

## Remplir ma semaine

A button in the planner. You say the direction — vegetarian, high in protein,
quick, cheap, and any of twelve cuisines — and which meals you want filled;
dinner by default, because that is what a week is planned around.

**Your own recipes answer first, and for free.** A cook with thirty recipes
asking for something vegetarian never pays a cent and never waits on anything
outside this machine. A model is asked only for the slots your library could
not fill, only for an account entitled to it, and only while there is budget —
and each of those three refusals ends the same way: fewer proposals, never an
error. Offering to sell you a plan in the middle of answering your question
would be reading the room badly.

Nothing is written by proposing. Intentions add up (vegetarian *and* quick
means both), cuisines are alternatives, a draft is never proposed for Thursday,
and no dish appears twice — running out comes back as "four found, three slots
empty", which is an answer.

## Corriger la proposition

A first proposal that you have to take or leave is not a proposal.

Turn a dish down and say why, from a short list. Everything you kept stays
exactly where it is, and only the refused slots are asked about again — the
question is per **meal**, not per day, because keeping Tuesday's dinner must
not give up on Tuesday's lunch. A dish you refused never comes back: it travels
back by name as well as by id, since an idea has no id, and a suggestion that
reappears after being turned down is the fastest way to lose somebody.

**One of the four reasons actually does something.** "Trop long" adds *rapide*
to the next ask, and the chip lighting up is what tells you so. The other three
are honest exclusions — acting on "pas envie" would be inventing a rule nobody
asked for, and pretending to is worse than not.

Next to them is one free-text line — "moins de pâtes" — which reaches a model
and nothing else. It is quoted into the request as something a person said,
never as an instruction, with its own quotes flattened and 200 characters as
the cap. A page you import, a photo you take and a note you type are all
written by somebody; none of them gives orders.

Four rounds and it stops. A conversation that cannot end is not one, so what is
offered instead is the honest exit: keep what suits you, fill the rest by hand.

## De la semaine aux courses

One press from the accepted week to what there is to buy.

And on the list, a meal you already have everything for can be taken out of it
with one tick. The meal stays on the plan, keeps its figures, is still cooked
and still counted — only its lines leave the list. That decision lives on the
*meal* rather than on a list line, because the list is rebuilt from the week
every time it is opened, and a decision stored on a line would be taken again
next Monday. Every tick you made in a shop, and everything you added by hand,
survives it untouched.

## Cuisiner en une fois

The other half of the word "preparation", and the harder half.

**Sharing an ingredient is not sharing work.** Two dishes that both use one
onion do not get prepared together for that reason, and a set built by counting
every ingredient in common gives you four dishes "sharing" salt, pepper and
olive oil — a grouping that looks rigorous and wastes an afternoon.

So the rule is narrow and it is about quantity rather than names. Only units
you weigh or count carry a base — a tablespoon of anything is a seasoning by
definition — and only real amounts, which is what keeps a pinch of saffron out.
A group where no base serves more than half its dishes is dropped rather than
offered: three curries with nothing in common are not a batch.

What you get is **sets, not dishes one by one**, each saying what makes it one:
*3 plats sur 4 sont bâtis sur les mêmes 900 g de lentilles corail*, with every
base and its total. "These four, trust me" asks for faith. This can be checked
in a second. Recipes you had already tagged *batch cooking* come first, because
a person saying so is better information than anything computed.

Nothing found is an answer too, and the screen says so without calling your
library an error.

## Le plan de travail

The week you already planned, read as one afternoon.

The shared bases first — what gets prepared once, with the total to actually
cook and which dishes it serves. Then every dish, the longest starting, each
carrying only what is *left* once the bases are made. Repeating an ingredient
you already prepared is how a work plan turns back into six recipes side by
side.

It prints, on a page of its own with a box to tick beside each base. Two hours
of cooking happen with wet hands and a phone that has gone dark.

**And it never tells you how long anything keeps.** No published figure sits
behind a shelf life, so an invented one would be a health risk rather than an
approximation. The sheet says out loud that it does not know, and leaves that
judgement to the person who can smell the fridge. It is also not cooking mode —
that follows one recipe, one step at a time, with dirty hands. This is read
before you start.

## Under it

**The library answers before a model does, and usually instead of one.** Every
screen above searches what you already wrote first, and says out loud when it
did not. What a model returns is never trusted about itself: a set it proposes
goes through exactly the same arithmetic as one of yours, and a set that turns
out to share nothing is dropped rather than dressed up.

**One definition of "the same ingredient".** `ShoppingService.key` was already
the only answer for the shopping list; the batch stories ask the same question
of the same lines and go through the same function. A base counted under one
key and bought under another is a plan that disagrees with the list it was
built from.

**The library's filters fold at every width.** Eleven rows of chips behind one
control that says how many are on — hiding a filter is only allowed while it
still says it is set.

Three things worth writing down, because each cost an afternoon:

- A message key that is a **string in one place and an object in another**
  resolves to the object. The label rendered an error nobody sees until they
  print.
- A `readOnly` transaction fails on a brand-new account and on nothing else,
  because a household is created the first time anything needs one — the one
  case nobody runs by hand.
- Component tests that pass alone and fail in the full run are a scheduler
  talking, not a component that got slower.

## What is left

**Nothing about payment works yet.** No provider is chosen, so the screen still
says the plan is not open. Everything paid for remains open to everybody:
`app.subscription.enforce` is off, and reading an image, a page or a week with
a model is capped at 10 CHF a month per account and 50 across all of them.

**The backups still do not leave the machine.** `FUELR_BACKUP_POST_HOOK` is one
line, and the last half of that story.

**Batch cooking knows nothing about conservation**, and says so rather than
guessing. A real answer needs published data, and until there is some, the
screen keeps its mouth shut.
